## Abstract

PACT (Provenance Attestation and Chain of Trust) is an open protocol for establishing verifiable, privacy-preserving chains of institutional trust from cryptographic signatures already embedded in existing email infrastructure. PACT processes DMARC Aggregate Reports (rua=) — a standard reporting mechanism already generated by every major mail server in the world — to build an append-only Merkle tree of domain-level attestations, derive organic trust scores, and detect infrastructure anomalies, all without ever accessing, processing, or storing any message content, recipient identity, or personal data of any kind.

PACT is signature-primitive agnostic. The v1 implementation is sourced exclusively from DMARC aggregate reporting, the universally deployed email authentication reporting standard. The protocol is designed from the outset to incorporate S/MIME, DKIM2, post-quantum signatures, and any future signing primitive that meets the protocol's attestation interface.

The result is a decentralized domain provenance layer — the trust foundation on which applications requiring richer semantics (document verification, compliance certification, KYC, anti-spam) can be built. PACT itself requires no participation from senders beyond a single DNS field addition, no behavioral change from verifiers, no message content access, and no trusted third party to adjudicate authenticity claims. It is the TCP/IP of institutional email trust: narrow, blind to content, and universally applicable.

## 1. Motivation

### 1.1 The Broken Trust Model

Every institution in the world communicates via email. Every outbound email from an institutional domain is automatically cryptographically signed by the sending mail server using DKIM — a process that has operated silently and universally since 2011. This signature binds the sending domain to the exact content of the message at the moment of transmission. It is, in effect, an accidental notarization performed at global scale.

DMARC (Domain-based Message Authentication, Reporting, and Conformance) extends this further: every receiving mail server that validates a DKIM signature already generates a structured aggregate report describing that validation event and sends it to the domain owner. These reports are generated today, by billions of email deliveries, across every major mail server in the world.

Neither the DKIM signature nor the DMARC aggregate report has ever been systematically captured, persisted, or chained into a provenance layer.

The consequence is a world where:

- A payslip from payroll@company.com is indistinguishable from a fabricated PDF after the signing key rotates.
- A contract confirmation from deals@counterparty.com can be repudiated with no cryptographic recourse.
- A wire instruction from a compromised account at a legitimate domain passes every authentication check.
- An invoice altered between receipt and payment leaves no forensic trail.
- A domain impersonated by a lookalike (wise-payments.com vs wise.com) has no verifiable history that distinguishes it from the legitimate domain.

Generative AI accelerated this crisis. Documents that previously required skill and time to fabricate are now generated in seconds. In November 2024, FinCEN issued Alert FIN-2024-Alert004 explicitly identifying AI-generated synthetic financial documents as an active and growing threat to the US financial system. No corresponding technical standard exists to address it.

### 1.2 What PACT Provides

PACT provides three guarantees that no existing system provides simultaneously:

**Domain Provenance** — A verified, tamper-evident record that a specific institutional domain has been sending cryptographically authenticated email at a specific volume, from specific infrastructure, continuously over a specific period of time. This record cannot be fabricated retroactively.

**Anomaly Detection** — Statistical monitoring of each domain's authentication patterns derived from aggregate report data. Deviations from established baselines — unusual failure spikes, unrecognized sending infrastructure, lookalike domain activity — are detected and surfaced without accessing any message content.

**Organic Trust** — A mathematically derived trust score for each domain, computed from the volume, diversity, and maturity of its verified authentication history. No authority assigns this score. It emerges from observable reality.

### 1.3 The Privacy-First Constraint

PACT is designed around a non-negotiable privacy constraint:

**PACT never accesses, processes, transmits, or stores any message content, message subject, recipient identity, sender email address, or any personal data of any kind.**

This is not a policy commitment. It is an architectural guarantee enforced by the data source itself. DMARC aggregate reports contain only authentication metadata — domain names, IP addresses, DKIM pass/fail results, message counts, and time periods. They contain no message content, no subject lines, no recipient addresses, and no personally identifiable information.

PACT processes only aggregate reports. The privacy guarantee is therefore structural, not contractual.

### 1.4 What PACT Is Not

PACT is a domain provenance protocol. It is not a document authentication protocol. This distinction is fundamental.

PACT answers the question: "Is this domain a legitimate institutional sender with a verified history of authenticated email activity?" It does not answer: "Is this specific email or attachment authentic and unaltered?"

Applications that require document-level or message-level proof — legal evidence certification, invoice authentication, individual email verification — can be built on top of PACT, using the domain trust score as a foundational trust signal. These are separate products that consume the PACT protocol. They are not part of the PACT protocol itself.

This boundary exists for a reason. Domain provenance from aggregate reports is structurally privacy-preserving. Message-level proof requires access to individual message data — a fundamentally different privacy model that requires explicit user consent and is outside the scope of this specification.

### 1.5 The Layered Ecosystem Model

PACT follows the same architectural principle as the internet protocol stack: a base layer that is deliberately narrow, blind, and universal, on top of which applications with richer semantics are built.

```

The protocol conversation and the product conversation are entirely separable.

## 2. DMARC as the Data Source

### 2.1 What DMARC Aggregate Reports Contain

DMARC aggregate reports (rua=) are XML documents generated and sent every 24 hours by receiving mail servers to the domain owner. They are already being generated today by every major mail provider — Gmail, Outlook, Yahoo, and others — for every domain that has published a DMARC record.

Each report contains:

```xml
<record>
  <row>
    <source_ip>209.85.220.41</source_ip>
    <count>1247</count>
    <policy_evaluated>
      <disposition>none</disposition>
      <dkim>pass</dkim>
      <spf>pass</spf>
    </policy_evaluated>
  </row>
  <identifiers>
    <header_from>wise.com</header_from>
  </identifiers>
  <auth_results>
    <dkim>
      <domain>wise.com</domain>
      <selector>google-2024</selector>
      <result>pass</result>
    </dkim>
  </auth_results>
</record>
```

This contains: the sending domain, the DKIM selector used, the result of validation, the sending IP, the message count, and the reporting period. It contains nothing about the content, subject, recipients, or any personally identifiable information.

### 2.2 How a Domain Connects to PACT

A domain operator adds PACT as a co-recipient of their DMARC aggregate reports by adding a single address to their existing DNS record:

```
BEFORE:
_dmarc.wise.com TXT
  "v=DMARC1; p=reject; rua=mailto:dmarc@wise.com"

AFTER:
_dmarc.wise.com TXT
  "v=DMARC1; p=reject;
   rua=mailto:dmarc@wise.com,
       mailto:rua@webuildreal.dev"
```

That is the only change required. No software installation. No API integration. No SDK. No behavioral change for any sender or recipient. The receiving mail servers of the world — Gmail, Outlook, Yahoo — continue generating the same aggregate reports they always have. They now send a copy to PACT alongside the domain's existing destination.

### 2.3 Zero-Friction Onboarding

For domain operators who manage their DNS through supported providers, PACT offers OAuth-based onboarding that adds the rua= field automatically:

- Cloudflare OAuth: one confirmation click, no manual DNS editing
- Route53 OAuth: one confirmation click, no manual DNS editing
- Manual: one DNS field addition for all other providers

For domain operators already using a DMARC reporting service (Postmark, Valimail, EasyDMARC, Dmarcian), PACT can be added as a forwarding destination in those services' dashboards — zero DNS changes required.

> **Live MVP note:** Reference deployment onboarding is **Cloudflare OAuth**, **manual DNS**, and **existing-tool forwarding**. Route 53 / AWS DNS automation is deferred — see [pact_protocol_v02.md](pact_protocol_v02.md) §2.3 and root `README.md`.

### 2.4 What PACT Does Not Use

PACT explicitly does not use and does not request access to:

- DMARC forensic reports (ruf=): these contain message-level data. PACT's privacy-first design excludes them entirely. Any application requiring individual message verification must obtain that data directly from the user who owns the message — never from ruf= reports.
- Email content via IMAP, POP3, or any mailbox access protocol.
- Gmail API, Microsoft Graph API, or any mail provider API requiring OAuth scopes.
- Any message-level data whatsoever.

## 3. Protocol Operation

### 3.1 Data Ingestion

The first reference implementation (PBM Labs LLC), hosted under the we build real movement, operates a purpose-built SMTP receiver at rua@pact.webuildreal.dev that accepts incoming DMARC aggregate reports. (It also accepts legacy `rua@webuildreal.dev` and `rua@pact.pbm-labs.com` for domains that connected before the canonical intake.) Upon receipt:

1. The report XML is parsed and validated against the DMARC aggregate report schema (RFC 7489 Appendix C).
2. Per-domain authentication records are extracted.
3. The raw report is immediately discarded after extraction. It is never stored.
4. Only the extracted aggregate signals are retained for processing.

### 3.2 Leaf Construction

Each processed aggregate report generates one leaf in the PACT Merkle tree. A leaf represents a verified authentication event for a domain over a reporting period:

```
leaf = keccak256(
  domain_hash,      // keccak256 of the sending domain (e.g. "wise.com")
  period_start,     // Unix timestamp: start of reporting period
  period_end,       // Unix timestamp: end of reporting period
  dkim_pass_count,  // Number of messages with DKIM pass in this period
  dkim_fail_count,  // Number of messages with DKIM fail in this period
  selector_hash,    // keccak256 of sorted DKIM selectors seen in this period
  source_ip_hash,   // keccak256 of sorted /24 IP ranges seen in this period
  report_hash       // keccak256 of the original report identifier
)
```

keccak256 is used throughout — in the off-chain TypeScript implementation and in the on-chain Solidity verifier. Using the same hash function in both layers ensures that any Merkle inclusion proof generated off-chain is verifiable on-chain without a dedicated proof verifier contract.

No message content. No recipient identity. No personal data. The leaf is a cryptographic commitment to authentication infrastructure activity over a time period.

### 3.3 Merkle Tree and On-Chain Anchoring

Leaves are inserted into an append-only sparse Merkle tree. The Merkle root is published on-chain at regular intervals. Once published, the root is immutable — it cannot be modified or deleted.

Any party can independently verify that a specific leaf is a member of the tree by providing the leaf value and a Merkle inclusion proof (the path of sibling hashes from the leaf to the root). This verification requires only the published on-chain root and does not require contacting the reference operator.

### 3.4 Anomaly Detection

PACT continuously monitors the aggregate report stream for each connected domain and computes a rolling baseline of normal authentication behavior:

```
BASELINE PER DOMAIN (computed from historical reports)
  - Normal DKIM pass rate (e.g. 99.9%)
  - Known sending IP ranges
  - Known DKIM selectors in use
  - Normal daily message volume range
  - Normal volume distribution across receiving domains

ANOMALY SIGNALS (derived from deviations from baseline)

TYPE 1 — Failure rate spike
  Baseline: wise.com has 0.1% DKIM failure rate
  Alert: failure rate exceeds 3% in a 24h period
  Signal: infrastructure compromise or active spoofing attack

TYPE 2 — Unknown sending infrastructure
  Baseline: wise.com sends from 209.85.xxx.xxx (Google)
  Alert: DKIM failures from 185.234.xxx.xxx (unknown)
  Signal: unauthorized infrastructure sending as wise.com

TYPE 3 — Unknown selector
  Baseline: wise.com uses selectors google-2024, ses-2024
  Alert: new selector never seen in historical reports
  Signal: possible key compromise or unauthorized sending

TYPE 4 — Lookalike domain activity
  PACT monitors domains visually similar to connected domains
  Alert: wise-payments.com appears in aggregate reports
         with DKIM failures or low trust score
  Signal: active BEC campaign impersonating wise.com
```

All anomaly detection operates exclusively on authentication metadata from aggregate reports. No message content is accessed at any point in the detection pipeline.

Alerts are delivered to the domain operator via the notification channel they configure at onboarding (email, webhook, or API).

## 4. Organic Trust Scoring

### 4.1 Principle: No Registry, No Governance, No Curation

Trust scores in PACT are not declared. They are not assigned by a committee. They are not stored in a registry. They emerge organically from the accumulated, verifiable authentication activity of real institutional senders as recorded in aggregate reports from the global mail infrastructure.

A domain with millions of verified DKIM-pass messages from diverse receiving mail servers over years of continuous history cannot be fabricated retroactively. The cost of attacking the trust score is identical to the cost of operating as a legitimate high-volume institutional sender for years.

### 4.2 Two Independent Clocks, Never Merged

A domain has two distinct ages that PACT must never collapse into one number: how long the domain itself has existed, and how long it has been generating verified PACT history. These are frequently different, and the difference is meaningful rather than noise.

```
DOMAIN AGE
  Source: public domain registration record (WHOIS
  creation date, or equivalent public registry data)
  Meaning: how long this domain name has existed
  Verifiable independently of PACT, by anyone

PACT HISTORY
  Source: days since the domain's first processed
  aggregate report
  Meaning: how long PACT has been accumulating
  verified authentication activity for this domain
  Only PACT can attest to this
```

A domain that has existed for eight years but connected to PACT today has a long domain age and a PACT history of zero. This is common and expected — it describes every legitimate, established institution that has not yet connected, which on any given day is the overwhelming majority of the internet. It is not evidence of anything suspicious, and the protocol must not present it as if it were.

Collapsing these two clocks into a single maturity number produces a specific, serious failure mode: an institution with a decade of real operating history scores identically to a domain registered yesterday, on the day each one connects to PACT — because both have a PACT history of zero. This inverts the protocol's purpose. It would make an established acquirer of regulated infrastructure look indistinguishable from a freshly registered shell, for the sole reason that the established institution connected later. PACT must never produce that outcome.

For this reason, domain age and PACT history are reported as two separate, always-visible fields. Neither is hidden behind the other, and neither is averaged into a single opaque number that obscures which one a given domain actually has.

### 4.3 Trust Score Formula

The trust score itself is derived only from verified PACT history — volume, diversity, and the PACT-history maturity factor defined below. Domain age is never an input to the score's arithmetic. It is reported alongside the score, as context, precisely because it cannot be allowed to inflate a number that is supposed to mean "verified by PACT."

For a given domain d at time t:

```
T(d, t) = log(V(d,t) + 1) x D(d,t) x A(d,t)

V(d,t) = total DKIM-pass message count for domain d
          up to time t (from aggregate reports)

D(d,t) = unique receiving mail server count for d
          / total leaf count for d
         (diversity coefficient, range 0 to 1)
         Higher when messages are received by many
         independent mail servers globally

A(d,t) = 1 - e^(-lambda x pact_age(d,t))
         (PACT-history maturity factor, range 0 to 1)
         pact_age(d,t) = days since first aggregate
                    report received for domain d
         lambda = 0.005 (subject to calibration)
```

This is unchanged from the prior specification in its arithmetic — the only change is that `age(d,t)` is renamed `pact_age(d,t)` throughout the implementation, to make explicit in code and in every interface that this is PACT-verified time, not domain age, and the two must never be confused at the variable-naming level either.

**Logarithmic volume:** bulk anchor farming has sub-linear returns.

**Diversity coefficient:** a domain whose messages are received and reported by hundreds of independent mail servers globally has higher trust than one whose messages are concentrated in a single provider. This reflects real institutional reach.

**PACT-history maturity factor:** a domain that connected to PACT yesterday cannot reach a high maturity score regardless of message volume. This remains the primary defense against lookalike domain attacks and against domain hijacking (Section 5.3 below) — an attacker cannot fake years of PACT-verified history no matter how old the underlying domain registration is. Critically, this factor must not be inflated by domain age, or the hijacking defense in Section 5.3 collapses: a hijacker who seizes a domain with eight years of registration history would otherwise inherit a high maturity score on day one of the hijack, which is exactly the outcome the hijacking defense exists to prevent.

### 4.4 Trust Score Interpretation

```
T < 1.0    No verified PACT history yet — this includes
           both brand-new domains and decades-old
           institutions that simply haven't connected
T 1-3      Early PACT history — early-stage signal
T 3-6      Established PACT history — moderate confidence
T 6-9      Long, high-volume PACT history — high confidence
T > 9      Extensive PACT history — maximum confidence
           (e.g. wise.com, stripe.com, chase.com after
            years of millions of verified messages)
```

Applications using PACT define their own acceptance thresholds. The protocol does not enforce any threshold.

A score in the `T < 1.0` range must always be displayed together with the domain's registration age (Section 4.2), never alone. "No verified PACT history yet — domain registered 2017" and "No verified PACT history yet — domain registered this month" describe the same trust score and two entirely different situations. Any interface presenting a trust score without also presenting domain age is presenting an incomplete and potentially misleading picture.

### 4.5 Presenting the Score to Humans

The raw value of `T` is mathematically correct but not legible on its own. `T = 0.023` carries no information for a person with no reference point for what the maximum realistic value looks like — unlike a consumer credit score, which is deliberately scaled (e.g. 300–850) so that any value in the range is immediately interpretable. PACT must apply the same discipline: the underlying formula in Section 4.3 is never altered for presentation purposes, but a separate display layer maps the raw score onto a bounded, human-legible scale before it is ever shown to a person.

**The raw formula and the display scale are two different things and must never be conflated.** The formula stays exactly as specified in Section 4.3 — logarithmic volume, a 0–1 diversity ratio, an exponential maturity factor. The display scale is a presentation-only transform applied on top, using the band thresholds already defined in Section 4.4:

```
DISPLAY MAPPING (raw T → 0-100 shown to humans)

  T = 0          →  0 / 100   "No history yet"
                                (genuinely zero signal —
                                 e.g. connected, zero DKIM
                                 passes recorded so far)

  0 < T < 1.0    →  1-10 / 100  "Provisional"
                                (any nonzero signal, however
                                 small, must never display
                                 as 0 — a domain with real,
                                 verified reports is not in
                                 the same state as a domain
                                 with none)

  T 1-3          →  10-35 / 100  "Early"
  T 3-6          →  35-65 / 100  "Established"
  T 6-9          →  65-90 / 100  "High confidence"
  T > 9          →  90-100 / 100 "Maximum confidence"
```

The distinction between `T = 0` and `0 < T < 1.0` is not cosmetic. A domain that has connected and is already accumulating genuine, verified reports is in a fundamentally different state from one that has not produced a single verified event — even though both currently fall under the same "no meaningful confidence yet" interpretation band in Section 4.4. The display layer must never round a nonzero raw score down to a 0/100 display value, and the label shown for any nonzero score must be **"Provisional,"** never **"No history yet"** — the latter label is reserved exclusively for `T = 0`.

**Algorithm versioning:** Raw score continues to use algorithm id `pact-score-0.1`. Display mapping version MAY be tracked separately (e.g. `pact-display-0.1`) if band boundaries change in a future spec revision — without recomputing stored raw scores.

### 4.6 Communicating Progress, Not Just Position

The maturity factor's slow growth (Section 4.3, λ = 0.005, reaching its ceiling over roughly two years) is a deliberate security property, not a UX defect — see Section 5 (Proof of Operational Work) for why this specific slowness is what makes the score resistant to fabrication. A domain cannot be shown a meaningfully higher score sooner without weakening the same defense that makes the score worth trusting in the first place. This tradeoff is not negotiable, and no display change should attempt to compensate for it by adjusting λ or any other term in the formula itself.

What can and should be communicated is *progress toward the next interpretation band*, since a static low number observed repeatedly over weeks reads, to a human, as a broken or stalled system rather than as a slowly and correctly accumulating one. Any interface displaying a trust score in the `T < 3` range should also display:

```
- How long the domain has been accumulating PACT
  history (pact_age in days, distinct from domain
  registration age per Section 4.2)
- An estimate of the time remaining until the domain's
  current trajectory crosses into the next interpretation
  band, computed from the domain's current volume and
  diversity trend, not a fixed constant
```

This does not change what is true about the domain. It changes whether a person looking at the page understands that a low score today is the system working as intended on day nine, rather than evidence that something has failed.

## 5. Privacy Model

### 5.1 Structural Privacy Guarantee

PACT's privacy guarantee is structural, not policy-based. The guarantee holds because the data source — DMARC aggregate reports — contains no personal data. This cannot be changed by policy, misconfiguration, or breach, because the data never enters the system.

### 5.2 What Is On-Chain (Public)

Each Merkle leaf commits to:
- The sending domain (e.g. "wise.com") — already public in DNS
- The reporting period (start and end timestamps)
- Aggregate DKIM pass and fail counts
- Hashed selector and IP range (opaque to observers)
- A hash of the original report (for auditability)

### 5.3 What Is Never in the System

- Any message content (subject, body, attachments)
- Any recipient email address or identity
- Any sender email address (only the domain)
- Any personally identifiable information
- Any individual message identifier

### 5.4 GDPR Compliance

PACT processes only domain-level authentication metadata that is explicitly designed for third-party consumption under the DMARC standard (RFC 7489). Domain names are not personal data under GDPR. No data subject rights apply to the information PACT processes. No data processing agreements are required for the core protocol operation.

## 6. Domain Onboarding

### 6.1 The DNS Change

A domain connects to PACT by adding rua@webuildreal.dev as a co-recipient in its DMARC DNS record. This is a standard DMARC mechanism explicitly defined in RFC 7489 — a domain may specify multiple rua= destinations separated by commas.

The domain's existing DMARC policy, reporting destinations, and email operations are entirely unaffected. PACT receives a copy of the same reports that are already being sent to the domain's existing destinations.

### 6.2 External Destination Verification

RFC 7489 requires that third-party report destinations (domains other than the domain being reported on) verify their willingness to receive reports. PACT satisfies this by publishing a verification DNS record:

```
_report._dmarc.webuildreal.dev TXT
  "v=DMARC1"
```

This record is published once by the first reference implementation (PBM Labs LLC), hosted under the we build real movement, and authorizes all domains to send their DMARC aggregate reports to the reference intake. No per-domain configuration is required on the PACT side.

### 6.3 Onboarding Paths

Three onboarding paths are supported, in order of friction:

**Path A — OAuth (lowest friction)**
Domain operator connects their DNS provider (Cloudflare, Route53) via OAuth. PACT reads the existing _dmarc record and adds the rua= field automatically. One confirmation click. No manual editing.

**Path B — DMARC service forwarding (zero DNS changes)**
Domain operator adds PACT as a forwarding destination in their existing DMARC reporting service (Postmark, Valimail, EasyDMARC, Dmarcian). One click in an existing dashboard. No DNS changes.

**Path C — Manual DNS edit**
Domain operator adds rua@webuildreal.dev to the rua= field of their existing _dmarc TXT record. One field edit. Applicable to all DNS providers.

> **Live MVP note:** Shipped paths are Cloudflare OAuth, manual DNS, and existing-tool forwarding. Route 53 automation remains deferred (match v0.2 / root README).

## 7. BEC Attack Coverage

### 7.1 What PACT Detects via Aggregate Reports

```
ATTACK TYPE              DETECTION MECHANISM
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Domain Spoofing          Lookalike domain monitoring.
(wise-payments.com)      New domain has zero PACT history.
                         Trust score: zero or near-zero.
                         Maturity factor: reflects recent
                         registration date.
                         Gap vs wise.com score: immediate
                         and mathematically irrefutable.

Infrastructure Attack    Failure rate spike detection.
(Unauthorized sending)   Unknown IP ranges in aggregate
                         reports triggering anomaly alerts.
                         Domain operator notified within
                         24 hours of report receipt.

Key Compromise           Unknown DKIM selector appearing
                         in aggregate reports.
                         Alert to domain operator.

Volume Anomaly           Unusual message volume deviations
                         from established baseline.
```

### 7.2 What PACT Does Not Detect via Aggregate Reports

Account Takeover (BEC Modality 2): when an attacker compromises a legitimate email account and sends from a legitimate domain with valid DKIM, the aggregate report shows a DKIM pass. PACT cannot distinguish this from legitimate traffic at the aggregate level.

This is an honest limitation of the privacy-first aggregate-only design. Detection of individual message-level anomalies would require access to message content or metadata, which PACT's privacy model explicitly prohibits.

The domain-level trust score and anomaly detection that PACT provides address the two most common BEC vectors (domain spoofing and infrastructure attacks) while remaining fully privacy-preserving.

### 7.3 Complete BEC Coverage Summary

```
BEC Modality             PACT v1 Coverage
─────────────────────────────────────────
Domain Spoofing          Complete
Infrastructure Attack    Complete (24h detection)
Account Takeover         Not covered (by design —
                         privacy constraint)
Document Manipulation    Out of scope — requires
                         message-level access.
                         Addressable by applications
                         built on top of PACT with
                         explicit user consent.
```

## 8. Building on PACT Protocol

PACT Protocol provides domain provenance as a base layer. It deliberately stops at the domain level. Three named applications are defined in this ecosystem, each consuming PACT Protocol as infrastructure. They are not part of this specification.

### 8.1 The Protocol Boundary

PACT Protocol's scope ends at domain-level attestation derived from DMARC aggregate reports. Anything requiring access to individual messages, email content, attachments, or recipient identity is outside the protocol and must be implemented as a separate application with its own privacy model and explicit user consent mechanisms.

This boundary is not a limitation. It is the design. A protocol that never touches message content can make structural privacy guarantees that no policy or audit can override.

### 8.2 PACT Proof

PACT Proof is a document-level authentication application built on top of PACT Protocol. It addresses the use case that PACT Protocol deliberately excludes: proving that a specific email and its attachments are authentic and unaltered.

The user voluntarily exports a specific received email from their own inbox and provides it directly to PACT Proof. The application validates the DKIM signature of that individual email, generates a cryptographic certificate of authenticity for that message and its attachments, and references the sending domain's PACT Protocol trust score as a corroborating signal.

PACT Proof requires explicit, per-action user consent. It is never automatic and never passive. The user is always the source of the message data. No interception mechanism is used.

Use cases: legal evidence certification, invoice authentication, employment verification, income documentation for financial institutions.

### 8.3 PACT Signal

PACT Signal is a real-time anomaly detection application built on top of PACT Protocol. It surfaces the statistical deviations that PACT Protocol's aggregate report stream makes visible — failure rate spikes, unrecognized sending infrastructure, lookalike domain activity — and delivers them as actionable alerts to domain operators.

PACT Signal operates entirely on DMARC aggregate report metadata. It accesses no message content. It identifies no individual recipients or senders. It is an analytics layer over the same privacy-preserving data stream that PACT Protocol processes.

Use cases: active BEC campaign detection, domain impersonation alerting, DKIM infrastructure monitoring, key rotation anomaly detection.

### 8.4 PACT Chain

PACT Chain is a compliance and onboarding credential built on top of PACT Protocol. It packages the full Merkle inclusion proof and trust history of a connected domain into a portable, independently verifiable document suitable for regulatory submissions, financial institution onboarding, and audit trails.

A PACT Chain credential answers the question that banks, regulators, and counterparties increasingly need answered: "Has this institutional domain been operating with verified, authenticated email activity for a credible period of time?" The credential references the on-chain Merkle roots directly — any party can verify it without contacting the reference operator.

Use cases: corporate account opening at financial institutions, vendor onboarding for procurement compliance, regulatory filings requiring proof of institutional legitimacy, due diligence in M&A and investment processes.

### 8.5 Shared Constraints for All Applications

All applications built on PACT Protocol that go beyond domain-level provenance must:

- Require explicit, per-action user consent before accessing any individual message.
- Never operate passively or automatically on message content.
- Implement their own privacy model appropriate to their data access level.
- Never use automated or passive message interception — any individual message data must come directly and explicitly from the user who owns that message.
- Reference PACT Protocol domain trust scores as supplementary signals, not as standalone proof of individual message authenticity.

## 9. Smart Contract Specification

The PACT smart contract is minimal by design. It records Merkle roots and entity statistics. It does not compute trust scores. It does not maintain registries. All trust scoring and anomaly detection is done off-chain by reading the contract's public state.

### 9.1 State

```solidity
// Append-only array of published Merkle roots.
// This is the only state the contract maintains.
// All domain statistics and trust scoring are computed
// off-chain by reading the public leaf data in Supabase
// and verifying against these on-chain roots.
struct MerkleRoot {
    bytes32 root;
    uint256 timestamp;
    uint256 leafCount;
}
MerkleRoot[] public roots;
```

### 9.2 Functions

```solidity
function publishRoot(
    bytes32 root,
    uint256 leafCount
) external;

function verifyProof(
    bytes32 leaf,
    bytes32[] calldata proof,
    bytes32 root
) external pure returns (bool);

// Convenience getter for the most recently published root.
function getLatestRoot()
    external
    view
    returns (bytes32 root, uint256 leafCount, uint256 timestamp);
```

### 9.3 Governance

The smart contract has no upgrade mechanism. It is deployed once and is immutable. Root publication is initially permissioned to first-reference-implementation nodes operated by PBM Labs LLC. Permissionless operation with economic stake requirements is deferred to v0.2.

## 10. Threat Model

### 10.1 Assumptions

PACT assumes:
- The underlying blockchain is censorship-resistant and provides immutable transaction ordering.
- keccak256 is collision-resistant.
- DMARC aggregate reports are generated honestly by receiving mail servers.
- The domain operator who adds PACT to their rua= field controls that domain.

### 10.2 Out of Scope

PACT does not protect against:
- A domain operator who deliberately sends fraudulent email from their own domain.
- Receiving mail servers that generate false aggregate reports.
- Account Takeover attacks (by design — privacy constraint).
- Domains that have not connected to PACT.

### 10.3 Attack Resistance

**Trust score gaming:** generating false DKIM-pass volume requires controlling the sending domain's private key and sending real email received by real mail servers. The cost equals the cost of legitimate operation.

**Fake aggregate reports:** PACT verifies that incoming reports originate from legitimate mail server infrastructure before processing. Reports from unknown sources are discarded.

**Retroactive fabrication:** the Merkle tree is append-only and on-chain. No leaf can be inserted with a past timestamp. Historical trust cannot be fabricated.

## 11. Relation to Existing Standards

**RFC 6376 (DKIM):** PACT uses DKIM authentication results as reported in DMARC aggregate reports. Fully compatible. No changes to DKIM infrastructure required.

**RFC 7489 (DMARC):** PACT is built on DMARC aggregate reporting. It uses the existing rua= mechanism exactly as specified. No changes to DMARC infrastructure required. PACT is a consumer of DMARC reports, not an extension of DMARC.

**RFC 8460 (SMTP TLS Reporting):** PACT is complementary to TLS-RPT. Both operate at the infrastructure metadata level without message content access.

**RFC 5751 (S/MIME):** Applicable to applications built on top of PACT that require individual message-level attestation with explicit user consent. Not part of the core PACT protocol.

**WebAuthn / FIDO2:** The optional Passkey sender extension uses standard WebAuthn without proprietary extensions.

**NIST PQC Standards (2024):** PACT's primitive-agnostic architecture accommodates post-quantum primitives in future versions.

**Federal Rules of Evidence 902(13) and 902(14):** The domain-level Chain credential generated from PACT Protocol history is designed to support declarations under FRE 902(13) (electronic process or system). PACT Proof — the Layer 2 application described in Section 8.2 — generates individual message certificates designed to support FRE 902(14) declarations. Legal counsel should confirm admissibility in specific jurisdictions.

**GDPR and privacy regulations:** PACT processes only domain-level authentication metadata. No personal data is processed or stored. No data subject rights apply to the information in PACT's data pipeline.

## 12. Open Questions for Community Input

1. **Leaf granularity:** the current design generates one leaf per aggregate report per domain (24-hour periods). A finer granularity (per sending IP, per selector) would provide richer anomaly detection at the cost of larger tree size.

2. **Proof system selection:** Groth16 requires a trusted setup. PLONK and STARKs eliminate this requirement at the cost of larger proofs. The aggregate-only core protocol does not currently require an on-chain ZK circuit — Merkle inclusion proofs are sufficient. Future versions may introduce ZK proofs for enhanced privacy of aggregate statistics.

3. **Root publication frequency:** daily publication aligned with aggregate report cadence is the natural default. More frequent publication is possible but increases gas costs without proportional benefit given the 24-hour report window.

4. **Permissionless node operation:** deferred to v0.2. Requires economic security model and stake slashing mechanism.

5. **Cross-chain deployment:** PACT roots could be published on multiple chains for redundancy. Canonical chain for dispute resolution requires community consensus.

6. **Trust score parameter calibration:** the lambda decay constant and component weights are initial estimates. Empirical calibration against real-world domain data should inform v0.2 parameters.

## Appendix A: Glossary

**Aggregate report (rua=):** a structured XML document generated by receiving mail servers and sent to domain owners every 24 hours, containing authentication metadata for email received from that domain. Defined in RFC 7489. Contains no message content or personal data.

**Anomaly detection:** PACT's statistical monitoring of per-domain authentication patterns, derived from aggregate reports, to identify deviations that signal active attacks or infrastructure compromise.

**Domain provenance:** a verifiable record of a domain's authenticated email activity history, derived from aggregate reports and anchored in the PACT Merkle tree.

**Leaf:** a single entry in the PACT Merkle tree, representing one processed aggregate report for one domain over one reporting period.

**Merkle inclusion proof:** a cryptographic proof that a specific leaf is a member of a Merkle tree with a given root, without revealing any other leaves.

**Organic trust score:** a computed metric for a domain derived from the volume, diversity, and maturity of its DKIM-pass history as reported in aggregate reports. Not declared or assigned — emergent from observable authentication activity.

**PACT (Provenance Attestation and Chain of Trust):** this protocol.

**Primitive agnosticism:** the architectural principle that PACT's trust infrastructure is independent of the underlying signing mechanism.

**Privacy-first design:** PACT's structural guarantee that no message content, recipient identity, or personal data of any kind enters the system at any point. Enforced by the data source, not by policy.

**Root of trust:** in PACT, a domain with a high organic trust score derived from accumulated authentication activity. Not appointed. Emergent.
